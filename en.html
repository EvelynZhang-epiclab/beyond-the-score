Beyond the Score Early Evaluations of GPT-6 Astra as a Robot Critic

Jinhao Zhang, Yuwei Zhang, Weitao Zhou

Simple AI

Gallery ↗ Code (coming soon) arXiv (coming soon)

Key takeaways

  • Astra distinguishes execution quality without task-specific fine-tuning. Zero-shot ranking τa is 0.8166 and direct preference accuracy is 92.4%, the highest point estimates among the three general-purpose configurations tested here.
  • Context examples have mixed effects. Progress VOC rises from 93.6 to 94.2, while ranking τa falls from 0.8166 to 0.7953. In a bottle-opening example, higher progress correlation also accompanies a worse endpoint estimate.
  • Higher RoboFail accuracy comes with lower failure recall. Accuracy is 85.96% with five examples versus 64.91% zero-shot. False alarms fall from 18 to 2, but misses rise from 2 to 6. Backend consistency is unconfirmed, so the difference cannot be attributed solely to examples.
  • Most of the composite explanation-score gain comes from success recognition. ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499; about 89.3% of the gain comes from the correct-success term, rather than the failure-explanation term.

Overview

We study whether GPT-6 Astra can assess robot execution from visual observations. On RBM trajectory data and the AHA adaptation of RoboFail, we evaluate four interfaces: independent trajectory scoring, direct comparison of two trajectories, progress estimation, and subtask success classification with explanations. Tasks and observations are supplied through prompts, without fine-tuning for these evaluations.

Astra obtains strong execution-quality scores in this evaluation. Further analysis shows that progress correlation, ranking discrimination, failure recall, and explanation quality do not always improve together. We present the results by interface and examine these differences through individual cases. All experiments are offline; we have not tested whether critic feedback improves policy learning or closed-loop execution.

1. Study design

A robot critic may compare trajectories, estimate progress, or check whether a subtask has finished. These uses require different judgments. We evaluate them separately rather than summarize all of them with one score.

2. Trajectory quality and preference

For independent scoring, the model observes each trajectory separately and the scores determine a ranking. Astra obtains Kendall τa of 0.8166. This metric measures agreement with reference quality order; inversions and predicted ties reduce the score. In direct preference, the model sees both trajectories and selects the better execution, reaching 92.4% accuracy. Because the input interfaces differ, preference accuracy is not the accuracy of ordering independently computed scores.

Astra has the highest point estimate on all three metrics among the three zero-shot general-purpose configurations tested here. Relative to GPT-5.6-sol, the gaps are 0.0666 in ranking τa, 1.7 percentage points in preference accuracy, and 0.1 in progress VOC. The clearest numerical separation is in independent ranking. Repeat-call variance and confidence intervals are unavailable, so these differences are descriptive rather than significance claims.

2.1 Comparison with prior work

RynnValue-8B reports ranking τa of 0.675. The Robometer paper reports τa of 0.655, preference accuracy of 82.5%, and progress VOC of 94.8. Astra’s ranking and preference values are numerically higher, while its progress VOC is lower than this Robometer record.

These literature values were not rerun on our samples. RynnValue describes a 976-trajectory OOD suite, whereas this study scores 567 trajectories. External preference evaluation uses 500 different-quality pairs per OOD source; our study uses 1,192 fixed pairs with incompletely documented composition. Sample, input, and aggregation differences prevent a matched-protocol superiority claim.

3. Progress estimation and context examples

We also test demonstrations supplied in the request. In-context learning (ICL) does not update model parameters, and the example construction differs by interface. Progress uses one same- or similar-task training trajectory with six stage scores. Ranking uses three randomly selected high-, middle-, and low-score training trajectories, without requiring a task match. The middle reference score is not the test set’s suboptimal label. These are not equal-context comparisons, and no ICL preference result is reported.

3.1 Progress correlation and endpoint estimates

Across 105 successful trajectories, zero-shot Astra obtains VOC of 93.6. Predictions use only current and preceding frames and are compared with a time reference. VOC is reported on a ×100 scale and measures progress–time correlation, not completion accuracy. A task can remain in preparation for a long period before a late state change; semantic progress need not increase uniformly with time.

With single-trajectory ICL, aggregate VOC rises to 94.2 and time-reference MSE falls from 0.0402 to 0.0390. Per-trajectory changes are mixed: VOC improves on 58 trajectories, declines on 35, and is unchanged on 12. In the reported banana-transfer task, 18 of 19 trajectories decline and one is unchanged. Of the 58 trajectories with higher VOC, 11 have worse MSE.

The public terminal label for bottle-opening case T098 is success. Zero-shot gives a terminal score of 1.00, while ICL gives 0.35. ICL has higher VOC, but its endpoint score is farther from the public success label and its time-reference MSE is about 3.22 times larger.

3.2 Ties and inversions in ranking

With three reference trajectories, τa falls from 0.8166 to 0.7953. Of 41 source–task groups, eight improve, ten decline, and 23 remain unchanged. Under score-consistent pair weighting, inversions fall from 4.78% to 3.68%, but ties rise from 8.78% to 13.11% and correct strict orders fall from 86.44% to 83.21%.

Fewer inversions therefore do not produce a better ranking. More ties leave executions of different quality indistinguishable and offset the reduction in inversions. The decomposition explains the score change, but does not identify the mechanism by which examples affect judgment.

4. Subtask success and failure

RoboFail asks the model to classify subtask success from a keyframe montage and explain failures. Zero-shot detects 32 of 34 failures, giving 94.12% failure recall. It also misclassifies 18 of 23 successes as failures, yielding overall accuracy of 37 / 57, or 64.91%.

With five external image–question–answer examples, the model answers 49 / 57 correctly, or 85.96%. Correct-success judgments rise from 5 to 21, while correct-failure judgments fall from 32 to 28. The net gain of 12 correct answers consists of 16 additional successes recognized and four fewer failures detected.

The false-alarm rate on successful subtasks falls from 78.26% to 8.70%, while the miss rate on failures rises from 5.88% to 17.65%. The five-example condition recognizes success more often, but also misses more failures. The practical choice depends on the costs of unnecessary intervention and missed failures, which are not measured here.

Two limitations apply. The five demonstrations are reported as disjoint from test trajectories, which does not establish environment, embodiment, near-duplicate, or pretraining independence. Also, returned model identifiers differ despite the same requested model name and low reasoning setting. Backend snapshot consistency is unconfirmed, so the observed difference does not isolate a causal effect of ICL.

5. Failure explanations and composite scores

Composite ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499 in the five-example condition. This metric does not measure failure explanations alone. Under the AHA rules, a correct success answer receives 1; a correct failure receives stemmed ROUGE-L F1 against the reference explanation; and a label disagreement receives 0. Scores are summed and divided by 57. Evaluation uses the original English answers, not the Chinese paraphrases in this article.

R = (correct successes + summed explanation scores for correct failures) / 57

The correct-success term rises from 0.0877 to 0.3684. Its increase, 16 / 57 ≈ 0.2807, accounts for about 89.3% of the reported total gain of 0.3144. The failure-explanation term rises from 0.0969 to 0.1305, accounting for the remaining 10.7%. A score of 0.499 therefore does not mean that 49.9% of failure explanations are correct. Most of the gain comes from recognizing success.

The explanation term also depends on which failures are detected and on lexical overlap with reference text. It does not independently distinguish a correct paraphrase from a similarly worded but incorrect diagnosis. Assessing the stated cause requires additional semantic review and physical evidence.

6. Discussion and limitations

These results support further study of Astra for offline robot assessment: it distinguishes execution quality, tracks progress, and detects many failures in the evaluated samples. The effects of examples nevertheless differ across metrics. Progress evaluation should include endpoint judgments; ranking should include ties; classification should report false alarms and misses separately; and explanations need validation beyond lexical overlap.

6.1 Limitations

Scope. No policy is trained or controlled using the critic. We do not measure reinforcement-learning sample efficiency, online failure localization, recovery, or closed-loop success. Offline scores do not establish reliable contact-state reasoning, causal diagnosis, or reward-model deployment safety.

Statistics and experimental control. The 57 RoboFail questions share 15 trajectory groups, and ranking pairs may share trajectories and tasks. Repeat-call variation and sampling uncertainty are not quantified, so small differences are not established improvements. RoboFail backend differences and example-selection effects also require separate control.

Labels and cases. Qualitative cases were selected post hoc and do not estimate the prevalence of failure types. Label ambiguity, limited visual evidence, and model error can all affect outcomes. Without independent blind review, model rationales do not establish that a reference label is wrong. We retain the released labels.

Reproducibility status

The available materials provide evaluation counts, demonstration constructions, aggregates, and selected cases. Full independent reproduction still requires source and sample manifests, preference split composition, input-frame identities and resolution, complete prompts and demonstration identities, requested and returned model versions, run dates, output budgets and sampling settings, and parsing, refusal, retry, and missing-output rules.

Scorer versions, aggregation weights, tie conventions, and per-sample predictions are also needed for score audits and uncertainty estimates that preserve trajectory or source–task dependence. Completing the release would not itself resolve backend or example-selection confounds; missing materials are also not evidence that the underlying experiments are invalid.

6.2 Next validation steps

The next step is to repeat comparisons under a confirmed common model snapshot with fixed inputs and demonstration-selection rules. Independent annotation of completion and failure explanations should then cover occluded, ambiguous, and low-observability cases. Finally, feedback should be tested with a fixed initial policy, algorithm, and interaction budget, using a success measure independent of the critic. That experiment should report feedback cost and check for reward exploitation.
