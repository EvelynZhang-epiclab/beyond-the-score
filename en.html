Key takeaways

  • Astra distinguishes execution quality without task-specific fine-tuning. Zero-shot ranking τa is 0.8166 and direct preference accuracy is 92.4%, the highest point estimates among the three general-purpose configurations tested here.
  • Context examples have mixed effects. Progress VOC rises from 93.6 to 94.2, while ranking τa falls from 0.8166 to 0.7953. In a bottle-opening example, higher progress correlation also accompanies a worse endpoint estimate.
  • Higher RoboFail accuracy comes with lower failure recall. Accuracy is 85.96% with five examples versus 64.91% zero-shot. False alarms fall from 18 to 2, but misses rise from 2 to 6. Backend consistency is unconfirmed, so the difference cannot be attributed solely to examples.
  • Most of the composite explanation-score gain comes from success recognition. ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499; about 89.3% of the gain comes from the correct-success term, rather than the failure-explanation term.

Background: from action policy to critic

Several recent studies test GPT-6 Astra as an action policy. The RoboDojo evaluation examines direct robot action generation, Astranomy studies Astra as a native manipulation policy, and a cross-domain embodied evaluation covers direct control and cooperation with trained policies. These studies primarily ask whether the model can decide what the robot should do next.

Robot systems also need to evaluate behavior that has already occurred. A critic can assess progress, compare executions, recognize an unfinished subtask, or explain a problem. Such feedback could support trajectory selection, execution monitoring, and reward or preference supervision for subsequent reinforcement learning. Action generation does not establish these assessment abilities, so we evaluate Astra separately as a robot critic.

We study whether GPT-6 Astra can assess robot execution from visual observations. On RBM trajectory data and the AHA adaptation of RoboFail, we evaluate four interfaces: independent trajectory scoring, direct comparison of two trajectories, progress estimation, and subtask success classification with explanations. Tasks and observations are supplied through prompts, without fine-tuning for these evaluations.

Astra obtains strong execution-quality scores in this evaluation. Further analysis shows that progress correlation, ranking discrimination, failure recall, and explanation quality do not always improve together. We present the results by interface and examine these differences through individual cases. All experiments are offline; we have not tested whether critic feedback improves policy learning or closed-loop execution.

1. Study design

We evaluate critics at two scales: complete executions and their progression, and the outcomes and explanations of individual subtasks. The first requires trajectories with task descriptions, temporal observations, and quality differences. The second requires subtask questions, outcome labels, and failure explanations. We therefore use the RBM trajectory evaluation and the AHA adaptation of RoboFail.

1.1 Why these two benchmarks

RBM: execution quality and progression. Successful, suboptimal, and failed trajectories of the same task provide reference quality comparisons. Temporal observations of successful executions let us examine how predicted progress changes during a task. Our progress and independent-ranking evaluations use six sources from RBM-EVAL-OOD: 105 successful trajectories for progress and 567 trajectories in 41 source–task groups for ranking. We separately evaluate 1,192 fixed preference pairs; their exact split composition remains incompletely documented.

RoboFail: subtask outcomes and explanations. We use 57 image–question examples from the AHA release, grouped into 15 operation sequences, with 23 success and 34 failure labels. The model sees the original keyframe montage, judges the current subtask, and explains a failure. This complements RBM: ordering two executions does not establish that a critic can recognize completion of an individual step or explain what went wrong.

The benchmarks cover different critic interfaces, so we do not merge their results into one score. OOD refers to the RBM split; it does not establish that a general-purpose model never encountered related scenes in pretraining. RoboFail also has multiple adaptations. Our 57-question set is not the same support used by Guardian or PRIMO.

1.2 Why evaluate the abilities in Sections 2–5 separately?

Table 1. Evaluation questions and potential uses. We test offline prerequisites, not benefits in a closed loop.
Section Critic question Why it matters
2 · Quality and preference Which execution is better? Do independent scores and direct comparisons reflect quality? Demonstration selection, candidate selection, and preference supervision require discrimination between better and worse executions.
3 · Progress and context How far has the task progressed? Do examples change progress, endpoints, or ranking? Dense feedback describes the execution process, but time correlation can conceal stalls, regressions, or incorrect completion judgments.
4 · Success and failure Did the subtask succeed? How often are there false alarms and missed failures? Continuing execution or intervening involves different error costs. Accuracy alone can conceal more missed failures.
5 · Failure explanations When the label is correct, does the explanation identify the issue in the reference? Diagnosis and subsequent correction require specific evidence. A correct outcome label is not a reliable causal explanation.

1.3 Inputs and metric definitions

Table 2. Evaluation interfaces. Counts are not numbers of independent robot trials.
Interface Model input Evaluation support
Independent ranking Task text and eight frames from one trajectory; trajectories scored independently 567 trajectories, 41 source–task groups
Direct preference Two trajectories in one request; choose the better execution 1,192 fixed pairs
Progress estimation Task text with current and past observations; no future frames 105 successful trajectories, five evaluation positions each
Subtask diagnosis Original keyframe montage and question; output a label and explanation 57 questions from 15 trajectory groups

Independent ranking: Kendall τa, higher is better. Within a source–task group, the reference quality order is success > suboptimal > failure. For pairs included by the scoring protocol, a correct order contributes +1, an inversion −1, and a predicted tie 0. Reference-quality ties must not be given an invented order; their inclusion follows the scorer. A group score can be written as (C − D) / N, where C and D count concordant and discordant pairs and N is the protocol’s pair count. Ties remain in the denominator, so assigning every trajectory the same score cannot yield a high τa. Aggregation must preserve the scorer’s inclusion rules and weights. We retain reported aggregates rather than interpret weighted decompositions as raw independent-pair counts.

Direct preference: accuracy, higher is better. Divide correct choices by the 1,192 evaluated pairs and multiply by 100%. Both trajectories are visible to the model together, unlike ranking independently computed scores. τa is not accuracy: 0.8166 does not mean 81.66% of preference choices are correct.

Progress: VOC and time-reference MSE. The RBM definition of VOC uses Pearson correlation. For one trajectory, let pᵢ be the prediction at evaluation position i and tᵢ the time reference from normalized frame position. Compute their correlation; the article reports it on a ×100 scale, higher being better. MSE measures numerical distance from the same time reference, lower being better.

r = Σᵢ(pᵢ − p̄)(tᵢ − t̄) / √[Σᵢ(pᵢ − p̄)² · Σᵢ(tᵢ − t̄)²]
VOC = 100 × r
MSE = (1 / m) Σᵢ(pᵢ − tᵢ)²

Here m is the number of evaluation positions. Cross-source aggregation of per-trajectory scores still requires the original scorer’s weights; total sample counts do not establish equal trajectory weighting. The time reference is not a human annotation of semantic completion. High correlation can coexist with consistently low scores or a wrong endpoint judgment. Pearson correlation is undefined for a constant prediction and needs the scorer’s convention, not an invented zero. The five stored predictions and time references in the bottle example reproduce its reported VOC and MSE.

Outcome classification: accuracy, failure recall, false-alarm rate, and miss rate. Treat failure as positive: TP is a correctly detected failure, FN a missed failure, TN a correctly recognized success, and FP a success predicted as failure. Accuracy is (TP + TN) / 57; failure recall is TP / 34; false-alarm rate is FP / 23; and miss rate is FN / 34. All four are reported as percentages. They score the answers, not the robot’s execution success rate.

Failure explanations: AHA composite ROUGE-L, higher is better. A wrong label receives 0, a correct success receives 1, and a correct failure receives stemmed ROUGE-L F1 between the original English explanation and reference. For the latter, let L be the longest common subsequence length of the token sequences: P = L / generated-token count, R = L / reference-token count, and F1 = 2PR / (P + R), with zero for no overlap. Sum scores over 57 questions and divide by 57. The composite mixes success recognition and failure-text overlap. Section 5 separates them rather than treating the composite as explanation accuracy.

2. Trajectory quality and preference

For independent scoring, the model observes each trajectory separately and the scores determine a ranking. Astra obtains Kendall τa of 0.8166. This metric measures agreement with reference quality order; inversions and predicted ties reduce the score. In direct preference, the model sees both trajectories and selects the better execution, reaching 92.4% accuracy. Because the input interfaces differ, preference accuracy is not the accuracy of ordering independently computed scores.

Astra has the highest point estimate on all three metrics among the three zero-shot general-purpose configurations tested here. Relative to GPT-5.6-sol, the gaps are 0.0666 in ranking τa, 1.7 percentage points in preference accuracy, and 0.1 in progress VOC. The clearest numerical separation is in independent ranking. Repeat-call variance and confidence intervals are unavailable, so these differences are descriptive rather than significance claims.

2.1 Comparison with prior work

RynnValue-8B reports ranking τa of 0.675. The Robometer paper reports τa of 0.655, preference accuracy of 82.5%, and progress VOC of 94.8. Astra’s ranking and preference values are numerically higher, while its progress VOC is lower than this Robometer record.

These literature values were not rerun on our samples. RynnValue describes a 976-trajectory OOD suite, whereas this study scores 567 trajectories. External preference evaluation uses 500 different-quality pairs per OOD source; our study uses 1,192 fixed pairs with incompletely documented composition. Sample, input, and aggregation differences prevent a matched-protocol superiority claim.

3. Progress estimation and context examples

Section 2 compares quality across executions. Here we examine progress within an execution and whether context examples make assessments more reliable. Under the interface-specific conditions in Section 1, we analyze progress correlation, endpoint estimates, and ranking discrimination.

3.1 Progress correlation and endpoint estimates

Across 105 successful trajectories, zero-shot Astra obtains VOC of 93.6. Predictions use only current and preceding frames and are compared with a time reference. VOC is reported on a ×100 scale and measures progress–time correlation, not completion accuracy. A task can remain in preparation for a long period before a late state change; semantic progress need not increase uniformly with time.

With single-trajectory ICL, aggregate VOC rises to 94.2 and time-reference MSE falls from 0.0402 to 0.0390. Per-trajectory changes are mixed: VOC improves on 58 trajectories, declines on 35, and is unchanged on 12. In the reported banana-transfer task, 18 of 19 trajectories decline and one is unchanged. Of the 58 trajectories with higher VOC, 11 have worse MSE.

The public terminal label for bottle-opening case T098 is success. Zero-shot gives a terminal score of 1.00, while ICL gives 0.35. ICL has higher VOC, but its endpoint score is farther from the public success label and its time-reference MSE is about 3.22 times larger.

3.3 Ties and inversions in ranking

With three reference trajectories, τa falls from 0.8166 to 0.7953. Of 41 source–task groups, eight improve, ten decline, and 23 remain unchanged. Under score-consistent pair weighting, inversions fall from 4.78% to 3.68%, but ties rise from 8.78% to 13.11% and correct strict orders fall from 86.44% to 83.21%.

Fewer inversions therefore do not produce a better ranking. More ties leave executions of different quality indistinguishable and offset the reduction in inversions. The decomposition explains the score change, but does not identify the mechanism by which examples affect judgment.

4. Subtask success and failure

RoboFail asks the model to classify subtask success from a keyframe montage and explain failures. Zero-shot detects 32 of 34 failures, giving 94.12% failure recall. It also misclassifies 18 of 23 successes as failures, yielding overall accuracy of 37 / 57, or 64.91%.

With five external image–question–answer examples, the model answers 49 / 57 correctly, or 85.96%. Correct-success judgments rise from 5 to 21, while correct-failure judgments fall from 32 to 28. The net gain of 12 correct answers consists of 16 additional successes recognized and four fewer failures detected.

An image-free reference always answers “failure.” From the current label counts, it gets 34 / 57 correct, or 59.65%. Zero-shot is 5.26 percentage points higher, recognizing five successes while missing two failures. This label-count deduction shows why accuracy must be read alongside both error types, rather than failure detection alone.

The false-alarm rate on successful subtasks falls from 78.26% to 8.70%, while the miss rate on failures rises from 5.88% to 17.65%. The five-example condition recognizes success more often, but also misses more failures. The practical choice depends on the costs of unnecessary intervention and missed failures, which are not measured here.

Two limitations apply. The five demonstrations are reported as disjoint from test trajectories, which does not establish environment, embodiment, near-duplicate, or pretraining independence. Also, returned model identifiers differ despite the same requested model name and low reasoning setting. Backend snapshot consistency is unconfirmed, so the observed difference does not isolate a causal effect of ICL.

5. Failure explanations and composite scores

Composite ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499 in the five-example condition. This metric does not measure failure explanations alone. Under the AHA rules, a correct success answer receives 1; a correct failure receives stemmed ROUGE-L F1 against the reference explanation; and a label disagreement receives 0. Scores are summed and divided by 57. Evaluation uses the original English answers, not the Chinese paraphrases in this article.

R = (correct successes + summed explanation scores for correct failures) / 57

The correct-success term rises from 0.0877 to 0.3684. Its increase, 16 / 57 ≈ 0.2807, accounts for about 89.3% of the reported total gain of 0.3144. The failure-explanation term rises from 0.0969 to 0.1305, accounting for the remaining 10.7%. A score of 0.499 therefore does not mean that 49.9% of failure explanations are correct. Most of the gain comes from recognizing success.

The explanation term also depends on which failures are detected and on lexical overlap with reference text. It does not independently distinguish a correct paraphrase from a similarly worded but incorrect diagnosis. Assessing the stated cause requires additional semantic review and physical evidence.

6. Discussion and limitations

These results support further study of Astra for offline robot assessment: it distinguishes execution quality, tracks progress, and detects many failures in the evaluated samples. The effects of examples nevertheless differ across metrics. Progress evaluation should include endpoint judgments; ranking should include ties; classification should report false alarms and misses separately; and explanations need validation beyond lexical overlap.

6.1 Limitations

Scope. No policy is trained or controlled using the critic. We do not measure reinforcement-learning sample efficiency, online failure localization, recovery, or closed-loop success. Offline scores do not establish reliable contact-state reasoning, causal diagnosis, or reward-model deployment safety.

Suitability for reinforcement learning remains untested. We have not trained a policy with Astra rewards or tested whether its preferences produce a more effective learned reward model. Correlation and ranking on fixed offline trajectories do not establish reliability during policy updates, distribution shifts, or active reward seeking. This is a central question for the planned closed-loop experiments.

Statistics and experimental control. The 57 RoboFail questions share 15 trajectory groups, and ranking pairs may share trajectories and tasks. Repeat-call variation and sampling uncertainty are not quantified, so small differences are not established improvements. RoboFail backend differences and example-selection effects also require separate control.

Labels and cases. Qualitative cases were selected post hoc and do not estimate the prevalence of failure types. Label ambiguity, limited visual evidence, and model error can all affect outcomes. Without independent blind review, model rationales do not establish that a reference label is wrong. We retain the released labels.

Reproducibility status

The available materials provide evaluation counts, demonstration constructions, aggregates, and selected cases. Full independent reproduction still requires source and sample manifests, preference split composition, input-frame identities and resolution, complete prompts and demonstration identities, requested and returned model versions, run dates, output budgets and sampling settings, and parsing, refusal, retry, and missing-output rules.

Scorer versions, aggregation weights, tie conventions, and per-sample predictions are also needed for score audits and uncertainty estimates that preserve trajectory or source–task dependence. Completing the release would not itself resolve backend or example-selection confounds; missing materials are also not evidence that the underlying experiments are invalid.

6.2 Next: reinforcement learning with critic feedback

We plan to use Astra’s assessments in reinforcement learning to test whether evaluating existing trajectories translates into better policy learning. We will first verify model snapshots, prompts, inputs, and demonstration selection, checking stability through repeated calls and independent annotation. We will then fix policy initialization, the training algorithm, and the environment interaction budget while comparing feedback sources.

Reward and preference pathways. One path converts validated progress or success judgments into rewards for policy optimization. Another uses trajectory preferences to supervise a reward model for policy learning. We will compare the signals separately and test calibration rather than assume that a high offline score makes an effective reward. Whether failure explanations help recovery or data selection requires separate ablations, not an inference from ROUGE-L.

Controls and evaluation. Where available, controls will include sparse environment rewards, hand-written rules or sensor-based success checks, and reproducible learned critics. We will also compare context conditions and feedback interfaces. Evaluation will report success-versus-interaction learning curves, samples needed to reach a given success level, uncertainty across random seeds, and query count, latency, and cost. Final success must be determined by environment criteria or human review independent of Astra, rather than making it both the reward source and sole evaluator.

Errors in the loop. Reinforcement learning changes the states a policy visits and may exploit critic errors. We will check whether ineffective actions, apparent completion, or unnecessarily long behavior earn high rewards, and track how false alarms and misses affect learning and recovery. These reinforcement-learning experiments have not been completed; this article does not report policy gains or sample-efficiency improvements.
